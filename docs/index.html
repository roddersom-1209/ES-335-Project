What sets a smartphone's price in India: specs or brand?

We predict the Indian launch price of phones from their listed specs, chip and brand, and measure how much extra each brand charges for the same hardware.

1. The question

How accurately can a smartphone's Indian launch price be predicted from its listed specifications, chip and brand, and how much of the price is explained by brand rather than hardware?

  • What we predict: the launch price in India, in rupees (₹). The models work with the natural log of the price, because spec effects act as percentages and prices are right-skewed.
  • One row is one phone variant: a specific RAM/storage configuration of a model (iPhone 16 128GB and iPhone 16 256GB are two rows).
  • When a prediction is made: when a phone's specs, chip, brand and launch year are known but its Indian price is not, for example at announcement, or when a buyer judges a phone from its listed specs. No input is derived from the price or from other countries' prices.

What we can and cannot claim. Predictions describe typical launch pricing in this dataset, not a phone's "true worth" or today's street price. Coefficients are associations, not causes. Results apply to the brands, launch years (–) and price range covered.
Brands with few phones are uncertain.
Quality factors not in the data (camera quality, display, materials, software updates) are outside the model.

PCA finds new axes, called principal components, that are combinations of the standardised specs.
The first component (PC1) is the direction in which the phones differ the most, the second (PC2) the most remaining variation at right angles to it, and so on.
Each component's loadings say how much each spec contributes to it. PCA looks only at the specs, never at the price, and uses the training phones only.

4. Method in simple words

  • Log price. A spec adds a percentage, not a fixed number of rupees, and the log turns percentages into plain additions that a linear model can fit. It also tames the few very expensive phones.
  • Split by phone model. Storage variants of the same phone (128 GB, 256 GB, …) are almost identical. All variants of a model go to the same side of every split, so a model is always tested on phones it has truly never seen. of the phone models ( phones) were locked away as the test set.
  • Baselines. "Always predict the average price" and "predict the brand's average price" show what a model must beat.
  • Linear models. Ordinary least squares (OLS) gives one coefficient per spec and brand.
    Model A (specs + chip, no brand) estimates a phone's hardware value. Ridge and lasso add a penalty that keeps coefficients stable when features overlap (RAM and storage rise together).
  • Trees. A decision tree asks yes/no questions ("flagship chip?", "more than 6 GB RAM?"). A random forest averages hundreds of trees.
    Gradient boosting adds small trees one at a time, each fixing the remaining errors.
    AdaBoost also adds trees one at a time, but it gives more weight to the phones it predicted badly, so each new tree concentrates on them.
    Trees can find thresholds and interactions that a linear model cannot.
  • Tuning and testing. Every setting was chosen by -fold cross-validation on the training set, grouped by phone model. The test set was used once, at the end. Uncertainty comes from bootstrap resamples of whole phone models.

Preprocessing choices, and why

  • Target: the natural log of the price. Spec effects act as percentages, and a few very expensive phones would otherwise dominate the errors.
  • RAM and storage on a log₂ scale. One unit then means "doubling".
    Going from 4 to 8 GB is treated like going from 8 to 16 GB, which matches how prices step up between variants.
  • Chip groups. The different processor names were grouped into chip groups by maker and tier (for example flagship Snapdragon 8, other Snapdragon, Dimensity 8000/9000, Helio).
    Too many categories would leave most of them with only a phone or two.
    Apple's A-series chips are grouped with flagship Snapdragon chips, because only Apple uses them, so "Apple chip" and "Apple brand" would otherwise be the same column (see Limitations).
  • Small brands grouped as "Other". Brands with very few phones after cleaning (Sony and Huawei) form one group, so no brand estimate rests on a handful of phones.
  • Categories as 0/1 columns (one-hot encoding). Brand and chip group become one column per category.
    OLS drops one reference category (Samsung, other Snapdragon) because the columns of one category always add up to 1. Ridge and lasso keep all of them, because their penalty makes the solution unique.
  • Standardised specs for ridge, lasso and elastic net. Each spec is rescaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so that the penalty treats a spec measured in mAh the same as one measured in GB.
    Tree models use the raw values, because a threshold works the same in any unit.
  • No leakage. All encoding and scaling sit inside scikit-learn pipelines, so they are fitted on the training part of each fold only.
    The other countries' prices in the file are never used as inputs, because they would give the answer away.
  • Rows. Tablets, exact duplicates and phones without a storage size were removed (see the cleaning table).
    Phones whose India launch was confirmed were added back from a manual web lookup, and phones with a doubtful India price were flagged but kept.

Patterns in the failures.

  • Value lines are predicted too expensive. Almost all large misses are cheap or mid-priced phones from value-focused brands and product lines (POCO, Vivo's T series, Realme's C series, Infinix, Tecno).
    They offer a lot of RAM and storage for a low price, and the model only knows the brand, not the product line.
  • Our chip groups misjudge some phones. Older flagship-class chips (Snapdragon 860 and 870) in later mid-range phones count as flagship chips, so those phones are predicted far too expensive.
  • Some prices may be wrong. Several of the largest misses are Infinix phones whose prices came from our web lookup, and a few phones have a doubtful India price (see Section 2).
  • Ridge fails more widely. It gives each brand one premium for all its phones, so it misprices brands that sell everything from budget phones to flagships, most of all on phones below ₹30,000 (see the price-band chart above).

6. Inside the linear models (OLS)

Ordinary least squares (OLS) fits one straight-line effect per feature on log price, by making the squared prediction errors as small as possible. We fit two versions:

  • Model A: specs + chip, no brand. It answers "what does a typical phone with this hardware cost?", the phone's hardware value.
  • Model B: specs + chip + brand. The same plus one effect per brand (measured against Samsung).

Because the target is log price, each coefficient is a percentage effect: the change in price for the stated change in one feature, with every other feature held fixed. Brackets are 95% confidence intervals.
The p-value is the probability of an effect at least this large if the true effect were zero (below 0.05 = clearly different from zero).

How much do the features overlap? (VIF)

The variance inflation factor (VIF) says how much a feature can be predicted from the others: VIF = 1 means no overlap.
A VIF above 5–10 is the usual warning level.
Overlap makes single coefficients unstable (it is why the RAM/storage split moves between Model A and B), which is what ridge regression is designed to handle.

7. How we tested, and how reliable the numbers are

  1. Lock a test set first. of the phone models ( phones from models) were set aside before any modelling.
    All storage variants of a model stay together.
  2. Choose every setting with cross-validation (CV) on the training phones: split them into groups by phone model, fit on 4, score on the 5th, rotate, and average.
  3. Use the test set once, at the end, with the chosen settings refitted on all training phones.
  4. Measure the uncertainty with bootstrap resamples of the test phone models (whole models drawn with replacement).

    11. Conclusions

    12. Limitations

    • One price source. All launch prices come from one Kaggle dataset that does not document its sources. We verified prices only for the web-lookup phones, and phones have a doubtful India price.
    • Missing quality factors. No camera quality (only megapixels), display type, materials, foldable flag, water resistance or software-update policy. Part of a "brand premium" is really these.
    • Dropped phones. Phones without storage in their name were dropped unless an India launch could be confirmed, so Honor and Huawei are barely represented.
    • How Apple's premium is measured (the Apple-chip assumption). Only Apple uses Apple's A-series chips, so in our data "has an Apple chip" and "is an Apple phone" are always the same thing.
      A model cannot separate two columns that are always identical, so it could not tell how much of an iPhone's price comes from its chip and how much from the Apple brand.
      To solve this, we put Apple's chips in the same group as Qualcomm's top chips (the Snapdragon 8 series) and treat both as equally valuable "flagship" chips.
      So Apple's premium means: how much more an iPhone costs than a phone from an average brand with a flagship chip and the same RAM, storage, cameras, battery and other specs.
      If Apple's chips are really worth more than a flagship Snapdragon, part of what we call Apple's brand premium is actually the value of its chip, so the premium is somewhat too high (and too low if they are worth less).
    • Brand-specific chips. Tensor (Google) and Kirin (Huawei) are each used by one brand, so those premiums partly include chip value.
    • Coarse chip groups. A chip family can mix flagship and mid-range chips, and older flagship chips count as flagship.
      This causes some of the largest errors.
    • Small brands and "Other" have wide intervals.
    • Launch price ≠ today's price. Discounts and street prices are not reflected.
    • Associations, not causes. A coefficient compares phones that differ in one feature.
      It does not say what a maker would charge if it changed that feature.
    • Small test set ( phones), so test scores have wide intervals.
